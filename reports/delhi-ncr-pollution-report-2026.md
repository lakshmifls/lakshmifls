# Delhi-NCR Pollution Report

**Report date:** 13 November 2026  
**Region:** Delhi National Capital Region (NCR)  
**Focus:** Air pollution, sources, seasonal patterns, impacts and practical control measures

## 1. Executive summary

Delhi-NCR experiences severe and recurring air-pollution episodes, especially during the post-monsoon and winter period. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a major concern because it can penetrate deep into the respiratory system. The pollution problem is regional: emissions generated inside Delhi interact with emissions from the wider NCR and upwind areas, while weather conditions can trap pollutants near the ground.

Historical source-apportionment studies do not produce one fixed percentage for every source because results vary by season, location, methodology and study year. TERI-ARAI estimates for Delhi have generally identified transport, industry, biomass burning and dust/construction as major contributors. A DPCC compilation of studies also shows substantial variation among inventories.

A major policy development is the move toward a new high-resolution NCR-wide emissions inventory/source-apportionment exercise using 2026 as the base year. The project is intended to map sources on a 500 m × 500 m grid and improve targeting of interventions.

## 2. Major pollutants

Key pollutants monitored in Delhi include:

- PM2.5 — fine particulate matter
- PM10 — coarse particulate matter
- NO2 — strongly associated with combustion and traffic
- SO2 — associated mainly with sulfur-containing fuels and industrial sources
- CO — incomplete combustion
- O3 — a secondary pollutant formed through atmospheric chemistry
- NH3 — important in secondary particulate formation

CPCB's Delhi-NCR monitoring framework uses PM10 and PM2.5 for PM-based concentration trends and also reports an overall AQI based on PM10, PM2.5, CO, SO2, NO2 and ozone.

## 3. Major pollution sources

### 3.1 Transport

Road transport is a significant source of particulate and gaseous pollution. Important contributors include heavy-duty vehicles, two-wheelers, three-wheelers and cars. Vehicle emissions also provide precursor gases that can participate in secondary-particle formation.

### 3.2 Industry and power-related emissions

Industrial activity across NCR can contribute substantially to Delhi's pollution load. Relevant sources include industrial combustion, brick kilns, power-related emissions and other fuel-burning operations.

### 3.3 Road dust and construction

Road-dust resuspension, soil dust and construction activity are important, particularly for PM10. Dry conditions and strong winds can increase the amount of dust entering the atmosphere.

### 3.4 Biomass and waste burning

Agricultural-residue burning, household biomass burning and open waste burning can cause sharp short-term increases in particulate pollution. Their influence can be especially important during particular post-monsoon and winter episodes.

### 3.5 Secondary particulate formation

PM2.5 is not only emitted directly. Gases such as NOx, SO2, ammonia and volatile organic compounds can undergo atmospheric reactions and form secondary particulate matter. This is one reason why controlling only visible smoke sources is insufficient.

## 4. Regional nature of the problem

Delhi cannot be treated as an isolated airshed. TERI-ARAI work found that Delhi's own emissions accounted for about 36% of Delhi PM2.5 in winter and 26% in summer in the study's regional assessment. Contributions from the rest of NCR and upwind areas were also significant.

Earlier studies similarly found that pollution can cross administrative boundaries. Therefore, an effective strategy requires coordinated action across Delhi, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh, Rajasthan and other relevant upwind areas rather than Delhi-only measures.

## 5. Seasonal pattern

### Winter/post-monsoon

Pollution episodes generally become more severe because:

- Lower temperatures and a shallow atmospheric mixing layer can reduce dispersion.
- Calm winds can allow pollutants to accumulate.
- Biomass-burning episodes can add substantial particulate emissions.
- Secondary particulate formation can increase.
- Regional transport of pollutants can become important.

### Summer

Dust becomes more prominent because of dry surfaces, construction activity and stronger winds. Atmospheric transport can also move dust and other pollutants over long distances.

## 6. Health impacts

Long-term exposure to PM2.5 is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Short-term exposure during severe pollution episodes can aggravate asthma and other respiratory symptoms.

Higher-risk groups include children, older adults, pregnant people and individuals with pre-existing heart or lung conditions.

Reducing exposure during severe episodes—particularly by improving indoor filtration, limiting outdoor exertion and following official health advisories—is an important complementary measure while emission controls are implemented.

## 7. Existing monitoring and governance

Delhi-NCR uses continuous air-quality monitoring stations and the National Air Quality Index framework. GRAP provides staged emergency measures for worsening air quality.

However, source-apportionment results must be interpreted carefully. Different studies use different years, geographical boundaries, emission factors, meteorological conditions and modelling/receptor methods. Consequently, source percentages should not be treated as a permanent annual formula.

## 8. Recommended action plan

### Immediate — 0 to 12 months

1. Intensify enforcement against open waste burning.
2. Strengthen dust suppression and construction-site compliance.
3. Increase mechanised road sweeping where appropriate.
4. Improve inspection and enforcement of visibly polluting vehicles.
5. Accelerate public-transport and last-mile connectivity improvements.
6. Improve real-time public communication during severe AQI episodes.
7. Expand clean-air shelters/indoor filtration in schools and other sensitive locations where justified.

### Medium term — 1 to 3 years

1. Accelerate electrification of buses and high-utilisation commercial fleets.
2. Tighten industrial emission monitoring and fuel standards.
3. Reduce reliance on high-emission industrial fuels.
4. Improve waste collection and processing to eliminate open burning.
5. Implement construction-material transport and dust-control standards consistently across NCR.
6. Develop coordinated crop-residue management incentives and enforcement.
7. Build a unified NCR emissions database.

### Long term — 3 to 10 years

1. Manage Delhi-NCR as a single regional airshed.
2. Use the new 2026-base-year, high-resolution inventory for targeted interventions.
3. Shift freight movement toward lower-emission modes and cleaner vehicles.
4. Expand clean electricity and electrification of end uses.
5. Integrate air-quality objectives into urban planning, transport planning and industrial zoning.
6. Publish transparent annual source-apportionment and intervention-effectiveness reports.

## 10. Conclusion

Delhi-NCR pollution is a complex regional problem rather than a single-source problem. Traffic, industrial activity, dust, biomass/waste burning and secondary atmospheric chemistry all contribute, with their relative importance changing by season and location.

The most important strategic shift is from short-term, Delhi-only emergency actions toward year-round, NCR-wide emissions management based on high-resolution data. Success should be measured not only by temporary AQI improvements during winter episodes but by sustained reductions in annual PM2.5 exposure.
